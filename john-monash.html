Scene I
The Man
★
MONASH
1865 – 1931
General Sir John Monash, GCMG, KCB, VD

Born on 27 June 1865 in Melbourne to Jewish immigrants from Prussia, John Monash would rise to become one of the most brilliant military commanders the world has known and one of Australia's most accomplished citizens.

A polymath in the truest sense, he was at once an engineer, a soldier, a scholar, and a leader of men. He earned degrees in engineering, arts, and law from the University of Melbourne — a feat almost unheard of in his day.

✴ ✴ ✴
Scene II
The Engineer

Before the guns of war called, Monash was already a man of distinction. As a civil engineer, he became a pioneer in the use of reinforced concrete in Australia, constructing bridges, buildings, and public works across Victoria.

His engineering mind — methodical, precise, relentlessly logical — would later become his greatest weapon on the battlefield, where he planned operations like an engineer builds a bridge: every load calculated, every stress accounted for.

“I don't care a damn for your loyal service when you think I am right; when I really want it most is when you think I am wrong.”
— Sir John Monash

Scene III
The Soldier

At Gallipoli in 1915, Monash commanded the 4th Infantry Brigade under impossible conditions. Though the campaign ended in withdrawal, his leadership under fire marked him as a commander of rare calibre.

On the Western Front, he rose to command the Australian Corps — the first time an Australian had commanded a corps-sized formation in battle. What followed was nothing short of revolutionary.

Scene IV
The Battle of Hamel

On 4 July 1918, Monash orchestrated the Battle of Hamel — a masterpiece of modern combined-arms warfare. For the first time, infantry, artillery, tanks, and aircraft were coordinated in a single, unified plan.

He predicted the battle would last ninety minutes. It was over in ninety-three. The precision stunned the Allied command and became the template for every major offensive that followed, ultimately breaking the Hindenburg Line and hastening the end of the war.

“I had formed the theory that the true role of the infantry was not to expend itself upon heroic physical effort, but on the contrary, to advance under the maximum possible protection of the maximum possible array of mechanical resources.”
— Sir John Monash, Australian Victories in France in 1918
✴ ✴ ✴
Scene V
After the Guns

After the war, Monash was appointed to oversee the repatriation of Australian troops — a monumental logistical task he executed with characteristic efficiency and care for his men.

Returning to civilian life, he became chairman of the State Electricity Commission of Victoria, transforming the state's power infrastructure. He also served as vice-chancellor of the University of Melbourne and championed the construction of the Shrine of Remembrance in Melbourne.

Scene VI
A Life in Frames
1865
Born in Melbourne, Victoria, to Louis and Bertha Monash, Jewish immigrants from Prussia.
1891
Graduates with a Master of Civil Engineering from the University of Melbourne.
1895
Completes degrees in both Arts and Law. Joins the militia, beginning his parallel military career.
1914
Commands the 4th Infantry Brigade upon the outbreak of the Great War.
1915
Leads troops at Gallipoli, earning distinction for leadership under fire.
1918
Appointed commander of the Australian Corps. Wins the Battle of Hamel in 93 minutes.
1918
Knighted by King George V on the battlefield — the first such honour since the age of the Plantagenets.
1920
Appointed chairman of the State Electricity Commission of Victoria.
1931
Dies on 8 October, aged 66. Given a state funeral attended by 300,000 mourners.

Scene VII
Legacy

Today, Sir John Monash is remembered as Australia's greatest military commander and one of the finest generals of the First World War. His face appears on the Australian $100 note. Monash University, one of the nation's leading institutions, bears his name.

Field Marshal Montgomery later called Monash the best general on the Western Front and perhaps any front during the Great War. His methods of combined-arms warfare became the foundation of modern military doctrine.
